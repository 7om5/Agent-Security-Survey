    Abstract

1   Multi-agent LLM systems are increasingly deployed for complex, long-horizon

2   tasks or emerge as a natural consequence of agents interacting in the wild. Yet, they

3   often have significant security vulnerabilities: the flexible protocols that enable

4   task generalization also expose novel threats, from cascading prompt injection to

5   inter-agent collusion. Progress in defending against these threats has been slowed

6   by a lack of shared empirical infrastructure, which forces bespoke environment

7   development for every new defense and makes standardized comparison impossi-

8   ble. Existing evaluations address isolated threat models or single-agent settings,

9   but none jointly vary attack, defense, and architecture across realistic multi-agent

10  environments. To address this gap, we introduce ORBIT, a configurable evaluation

11  framework for empirical multi-agent security research, built on UK AISI’s Inspect.

12  ORBIT lets researchers configure communication topologies, memory, scheduling,

13  and agent roles. It supports four threat types (indirect prompt injection, misuse,

14  compromised agents, collusion) and four defense strategies (security prompting,

15  guardian agents, monitors, dual-LLM patterns). The benchmark suite spans five

16  scenario families covering browser use, computer use, agentic coding, customer

17  service, and cooperative allocation. Using ORBIT, we conduct controlled exper-

18  iments varying defenses, attacks, topologies, and models. Our central finding

19  is a gap in defense transferability across threats: per-action defenses (an LLM

20  monitor and a guardian agent) that cut a compromised agent’s attack success by

21  60 points on multi-issue coding give no measurable protection against colluding

22  agents, and none of the defenses we tested generalized over all of the attacks we

23  tested. We further use ORBIT to demonstrate security–performance tradeoffs and

24  interactions between architectural choices and defense effectiveness. To accelerate

25  empirical multi-agent security work, we make ORBIT available open-source at:

26  https://anonymous.4open.science/r/orbit-D1A9.

27 1 Introduction

28 Frontier LLM agents have become increasingly capable, autonomous, and long-horizon [Motwani
29 et al., 2026], now able to carry out hours-long software engineering tasks [METR, 2026], conduct
30 significant research tasks [Schmidgall et al., 2025, Wen et al., 2026, Epoch AI, 2026, Anthropic,
31 2026b], and complete complex computer-use work [Anthropic, 2026a, OpenClaw, 2026]. These
32 systems are increasingly deployed as multi-agent systems (MAS), networks of interacting agents
33 [Li et al., 2024, Xiao et al., 2024, Gottweis et al., 2025]. This shift is driven both by intentional
34 system design and by the emergent composition of independently deployed models into larger
35 systems [Anthropic, 2025, Lin, 2026]. As LLM agents proliferate throughout the economy, we will
36 increasingly see decentralized multi-agent systems, with many agents interacting through flexible
37 protocols across the internet [Moltbook, 2026, Tomasev et al., 2025].

38 And yet, progress in securing these systems lags the pace of development. Several new threat models
39 have emerged, including memory poisoning and tool-use failures, infection-style attacks, and viral
40 misalignment through subliminal prompting [Schroeder de Witt et al., 2026, Lee and Tiwari, 2024,
41 Motwani et al., 2024, Weckbecker et al., 2026]. Defenses that are sufficient in single agent systems
42 do not necessarily extend to multi-agent settings. Vulnerabilities in MAS are amplified through task
43 decomposition, partial observability, responsibility distribution, while security-relevant properties

45 New attack surfaces in multi-agent systems. Many threats are unique to or amplified by multi-agent
46 settings [Hammond et al., 2025], opening the field of multi-agent security [Schroeder de Witt et al.,
47 2026]. Task decomposition transforms a harmful goal into benign-looking subtasks across agents,
48 allowing a harmful goal to bypass safety filters [Jones et al., 2024]. Insider compromise sees a
49 malicious or compromised agent use its trusted position to exploit its peer agents and cause damage
50 through communication channels [Yu et al., 2026]. Further, collusion threats see agents covertly
51 coordinate at the expense of system-level objectives [Motwani et al., 2024].

52 The infrastructure gap. Despite increasing evidence of these vulnerabilities and early work to char-
53 acterize them [Krawiecka and de Witt, 2025]1, defenses have lagged. Limited studies have explored
54 defenses using memory vaccination [Peigné et al., 2025] and carefully designed isolation [Debenedetti
55 et al., 2025], but progress has been slowed by a lack of empirical evaluations for multi-agent security
56 [Sharma et al., 2025]. Most defense studies evaluate a single mechanism on a single attack type on
57 a custom-built environment, making results difficult to compare. Existing multi-agent frameworks
58 lack support for configurable topologies, threat-model parametrization, or integration with defenses,
59 leaving developers without principled guidance on safeguarding their systems.

60 This work directly addresses that gap by introducing ORBIT, a configurable evaluation framework
61 for empirical multi-agent security research built on UK AISI’s Inspect2. ORBIT (Figure 1) provides
62 configurable setups, flexible orchestration to run arbitrary MAS, new multi-agent benchmarks, and
63 robust evaluation and logging infrastructure. Our contributions are:

64 1. Configurable MAS evaluation framework: We introduce the ORBIT framework, which decom-
65 poses experiments into six configurable layers, setup, scenario, attack, defense, execution, and
66 evaluation, enabling controlled ablation. New experiments can be configured with a YAML file,
67 while adding new attacks, defenses, and scenarios only requires implementing a short protocol.

87 2 Background and Related Work

 88 Single agent and security benchmarks: Prior studies have benchmarked LLM or single agent
 89 capabilities on software engineering [Jimenez et al., 2024], multimodal computer use [Xie et al.,
 90 2024], tool-agent-user interaction [Barres et al., 2025] while cybersecurity benchmarks have focused
 91 on assessment of public vulnerability databases [Fan et al., 2020, Ding et al., 2024, Zhu et al.,
 92 2025c, Mei et al., 2024]. At the model level, prompt injection and jailbreaks [Liu et al., 2024,
 93 Andriushchenko et al., 2024a], data poisoning and backdoors [Fendley et al., 2025, Yao et al., 2024],
 94 and privacy threats [Das et al., 2025, Gan et al., 2024] are well documented. Moving from models
 95 to agents, follow-up work examines LLMs embedded into agent scaffolds [Zhang et al., 2024],
 96 deployed for tasks including software engineering [Lee et al., 2025], personalized agents in real-
 97 world deployments [Wang et al., 2026], and computer use [Feng et al., 2026, Kuntz et al., 2025]. This
 98 line of work has also formalized performance under different agent-level attacks [Zhang et al., 2024],
 99 built environments for indirect prompt injection [Debenedetti et al., 2024], and quantified multi-turn
100 misuse around planning-stage refusal and execution-stage failures [Andriushchenko et al., 2024b].
101 All of this work, however, evaluates a single model or a single agent in isolation: none of these
102 benchmarks varies the number of interacting agents, parameterizes the threat model, or evaluates
103 attacks and defenses jointly under one protocol.

104 Multi-agent risks and security gaps: Multi-agent systems introduce vulnerabilities that are unique
105 to these systems and cannot be attributed to LLM or single agent systems [Hammond et al., 2025,
106 Hagag et al., 2026]. These arise from interactions between agents, giving rise to failure modes
107 such as miscoordination, conflict, and collusion and are driven by agents’ incentives, information
108 asymmetries, network effects, selection pressures, destabilizing dynamics, commitment problems,
109 emergent agency [Hammond et al., 2025]. Prior studies in MAS security identify threats models
110 under two different paradigms—cooperative and competitive [Schroeder de Witt et al., 2026, Peigné
111 et al., 2025]. In cooperative systems, collaboration itself introduces risks by allowing error am-
112 plification, propagation of misinformation, and collusive behavior [Motwani et al., 2024]. Covert
113 collusion has been shown to emerge through steganographic communication channels and sometimes
114 unintentionally from reward misspecification [Mathew et al., 2025]. In competitive systems, agents
115 may participate in deception, persuasion, sabotage and destabilize joint outcomes [Qi et al., 2025].
116 Additionally, adversarial agents have been shown to increase jailbreak success by manipulating
117 multi-agent debates through iterative dialogue [Qi et al., 2025, Amayuelas et al., 2024]. These factors
118 encourage infection-style attacks as compromised single agents can proliferate harmful behavior
119 across networks [Gu et al., 2024] and incorrect information can be further amplified through interac-
120 tions [Lee and Tiwari, 2024]. Despite these risks, multi-agent security lacks the unified system to
121 experiment with system design and analyze security-performance tradeoffs and researchers incur an
122 additional overhead in cost and effort for setting up experimental infrastructure.

123 Multi-agent evaluation and defense: Evaluation of MAS has focused on taxonomy and failure
124 mode identification from MAS traces [Cemri et al., 2025], collaboration dynamics evaluation [Zhu
125 et al., 2025a], and zero-shot cooperation in novel situations [Smith et al., 2025]. These studies
126 primarily evaluate task performance, or at most a single attack type in isolation, and none varies
127 the dimensions this work treats as experimental variables, such as topology, memory visibility,
128 and defense placement. Recent studies have identified this gap and explored setups that evaluate
129 MAS as a system, allowing experiments with subcomponents [Emde et al., 2026]. However, the
130 evaluations are not security-focused and do not allow multi-task or distributed experiments, threat
131 model parameterization, and defense analyses. Studies have also begun characterizing security-
132 performance tradeoffs in MAS [Peigné et al., 2025], topology effects [Zhu et al., 2025b], but these
133 analyses need to be expanded to consider the multiple drivers of risks in MAS [Hagag et al., 2026,
134 Schroeder de Witt et al., 2026]. Defense against vulnerabilities in MAS is also an underexplored
135 area and current studies focus on plan-then-execute style architecture [Del Rosario et al., 2025,
136 Debenedetti et al., 2025], with early work exploring (probe-based) monitoring [Rose et al., 2026].
137 Joint experimentation with attacks, defenses, and system design remains underexplored, largely
138 because each study has required its own bespoke infrastructure, which is the cost ORBIT removes.

139 Multi-agent frameworks: Several frameworks and orchestrators target multi-agent settings, some
140 with benchmark integrations [Nakamura et al., 2025, Emde et al., 2026, Zhu et al., 2025b, An et al.,
141 2025, Ma et al., 2026, Smith et al., 2025]. These platforms support agent coordination and task
142 evaluation, but none combines configurable topology and memory with attack and defense registries
143 and standardized security metrics under a single protocol, so varying one of these dimensions while
144 holding the rest fixed currently means rebuilding the stack. ORBIT provides this combination as an
145 evaluation framework, orchestrator, and benchmark suite; Appendix E compares the feature sets in
146 detail.

147 Non-LLM multi-agent systems: Security questions in multi-agent systems predate LLMs, and it
148 is worth situating our setting against that older line of work. Classical research centers on trust,
149 reputation, and access control [Yu et al., 2013, Jung et al., 2012], resilient consensus in the presence of
150 malfunctioning or misbehaving components [Lamport et al., 1982, LeBlanc et al., 2013], and secure
151 coordination under bounded adversaries such as energy-limited DoS and F-local faults [De Persis and
152 Tesi, 2015, Ishii et al., 2022]. LLM-based MAS differ because they are driven by foundation models
153 that communicate through open-ended, unstructured, free-form protocols instead of pre-specified
154 interaction languages and APIs [Schroeder de Witt et al., 2026]. Guarantees built on fixed message
155 semantics, protocol compliance, or explicit coordination assumptions therefore may not transfer,
156 which is why the empirical, system-level evaluation this paper provides is needed at all.

157 3 The ORBIT Framework

158 ORBIT is a framework for conducting empirical multi-agent security research, built as an extension of
159 Inspect AI [UK AI Security Institute, 2024]. This section describes the architecture, the composable
160 experiment layers that enable controlled ablation, and metrics the framework provides.

161 3.1 Design Principles

162 Three design principles motivate ORBIT’s architecture:

163 1. Configurable evaluation. The framework ships with presets for common topologies, attacks,
164 and defenses. Any dimension—agent roles, communication topology, memory visibility, attacks,
165 defenses, scheduling—can be varied by editing a YAML file. This factorization enables controlled
166 comparisons by varying one factor (e.g., defense) while holding others fixed.
167 2. Realistic agentic environments. Agents interact with the same tools and environments they would
168 encounter in deployment such as browser interaction [Le Sellier De Chezelles et al., 2024], software
169 engineering in real open-source codebases [Jimenez et al., 2024], and desktop computer use [Xie
170 et al., 2024]. Each scenario runs in a Docker-isolated sandbox.
171 3. Pluggable threat models. Researchers can easily introduce new attacks or defenses without
172 modifying the orchestrator or scoring pipeline. This extensibility is critical for ORBIT to evolve as
173 new threats emerge.

174 3.2 Composable Experiment Layers

175 Evaluating multi-agent security requires controlling dimensions that single-agent frameworks do not
176 expose. ORBIT decomposes these into six layers (Figure 2). Each layer is a frozen configuration
177 object and an experiment is the product of one choice per layer. This factorization enables controlled
178 ablation along a single axis with all others fixed.

179 1. Setup. The Setup layer defines agents (role, model, system prompt, tools), a directed communication
180 topology over them, and a memory visibility policy. Topology is declared as a directed graph in
181 YAML; a topology builder reads this graph and wires agents using Inspect’s delegation primitives.
182 We provide pre-configured topologies(star, mesh, chain, flat). Memory visibility controls what each
183 agent observes about others across turns via six configurable flags (own actions, own reasoning,
184 others’ actions, others’ reasoning, accumulated instructions, goal visibility), implemented as a
185 model filter that intercepts every generate() call. Details in Appendix I.
186 2. Scenario: task and execution environment. ORBIT includes five scenario families spanning
187 coding, browser, desktop, customer_service, cooperative_alloc (Table 1).
188 3. Attacks. Each attack specifies a type, threat model (knowledge level, budget, persistence, number
189 of compromised agents), injection timing, and success criterion. Built-in types include misuse,
190 indirect prompt injection, compromised agent, and collusion. See Appendix G for details.
191 4. Defenses. Each defense specifies a type, invocation mode, scope, and fail mode. Defenses can
192 be composed, multiple defenses at different layers can be activated simultaneously. Built-in
193 classes include system-prompt hardening, stateless per-action LLM monitor, stateful history-aware
194 guardian agent, and isolation via the Dual-LLM pattern [Beurer-Kellner et al., 2025]. See details in
195 Appendix H.

196 5. Execution. Inspect’s react() runs an agent to completion before returning control. ORBIT
197 introduces turn_react(), which executes one tool call per invocation. This then hands back to
198 AgentScheduler, which maintains per-agent isolated state and selects which agent runs each turn,
199 supporting arbitrarily configured execution orders for different experiments.
200 6. Evaluation. The Evaluation layer conducts (LLM-driven) scoring on the completed logs, including
201 calculating metrics and automated validity checks.

202 3.3 Orchestrator

203 The orchestrator is an Inspect @solver that manages experiment execution in three phases: (1) com-
204 ponent assembly from registries, (2) pre-deployment modifications (e.g. compromised agent injection,
205 prompt vaccination), and (3) a per-turn loop where each turn proceeds through attack injection, de-
206 fense hooks, agent execution, output monitoring, attack evaluation, and halt checking. Figure 2 shows
207 the full execution flow.

208 3.4 Metrics and Scoring

209 A sample is one task instance (e.g. one BrowserART behavior, one SWE-Bench issue, one distributed
210 constraint optimization problem (DCOP) allocation problem); an experiment evaluates N samples
211 under a fixed configuration. ORBIT computes a three-tier metric hierarchy.

212 1. Per-agent: compromise score. For each agent i ∈ {1, . . . , n}, the judge assigns Ci ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}:
213 the agent refused the attack (0), planned attack-advancing steps but did not execute them (0.5), or
214 executed harmful actions (1).
215 2. Per-sample: propagation depth and attack success. For sample j with n agents, propagation
216 depth PDj measures how broadly the attack spread and attack success ASj indicates full success:

221 overhead we report the benign task completion rate (BTCR), the fraction of non-attack samples

225 4. Cooperative Allocation Metrics For DCOP, ORBIT computes ground-truth metrics. Let R∗ denote

226 the total reward under constraint-optimal allocation and Ractual the realized total reward under

227 agent behavior; let r¯coalition and r¯non-coalition denote mean per-agent rewards among coalition and
228 honest agents respectively. We report regret = (R∗ − Ractual)/R∗, the normalized welfare loss

229 relative to optimal allocation, and coalition advantage = r¯coalition − r¯non-coalition, where positive

230 values show coalition members extract more reward than other agents (i.e., successful collusion).

231 4 Task Suite and Benchmarks

232 ORBIT’s task suite spans five scenario families covering browser automation, agentic coding, desk-
233 top computer use, customer service, and cooperative resource allocation (hospital, jira_ticket,
234 meeting_scheduling); see Table 1. Most source benchmarks were designed neither for multi-agent
235 systems nor for security evaluation, so integrating them into ORBIT required both multi-agent adapta-
236 tion and attack-surface engineering. The adaptation follows the routes marked in Table 1: functional
237 decomposition factors a single agent’s capabilities across multiple agents, from orchestrator–executor
238 pairs to specialist-role teams, connected by configurable topologies and memory-visibility levels,
239 while multi-goal restructuring converts a single-task source into a multi-task scenario in which
240 concurrent agents pursue separate goals in a shared environment. Attack-surface engineering spans
241 all five families, equipping each scenario with its threat-specific hooks, such as injection points in
242 files and tool outputs, compromised-agent roles, and covert coordination channels (Appendix G).

285 5 Experiment Results

286 We use ORBIT to conduct controlled experiments across the multi-agent security landscape, varying
287 defenses, attacks, topologies, and models. Each experiment varies one dimension while holding others
288 fixed, exploiting ORBIT’s configuration-driven factorial design. From ORBIT’s full configuration
289 space, we select a representative slice for evaluation: four defenses (system prompt, LLM monitor,
290 guardian agent, and dual-LLM), three threat types (misuse, compromised agent, and collusion),
291 and four scenario families (browser, coding, desktop, and cooperative_allocation). Models
292 tested include GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Qwen3-235B, and Llama 70B, alongside GPT-4o and
293 two mini models on selected scenarios. Topologies include standalone, star, mesh, with optional
294 shared memory. Every condition pairs a harmful evaluation (i.e. with an attack present) with a benign
295 evaluation (i.e. a baseline without attacks activated) on an identical setup, allowing us to measure
296 both attack success (ASR, Adv) and task performance under each defense (Benign). In total, the
297 experimental matrix spans ∼90 distinct conditions; full details of scenarios, attacks, defenses, and
298 results are shown in Appendices F, G, H, and J.

299 Statistical reporting. Every rate carries a 95% Wilson score interval and every continuous metric
300 a 95% percentile-bootstrap interval (10,000 resamples). Where two conditions run the same task
301 instances, the comparison is paired and the interval is computed on the per-instance differences. The
302 LLM judge shows self-agreement of 95.4% on re-scored decisions and 92.5% with an independent
303 second judge (Claude Opus 4.8), over ∼1,300 decisions. Over a 100-sample stratified subset, the
304 judge agreed with human grading on 91% of samples.

305 Table 2 presents defense effectiveness across four representative scenario–attack pairs. We report one
306 empirical finding (1) and two demonstrations of the controlled comparisons ORBIT enables (2–3).

307 (1) Per-action monitoring does not transfer from compromised agents to collusion. On SWE-
308 Bench MI, the LLM monitor cuts the attack success of a single compromised agent from 72% to 12%,
309 yet leaves a colluding pair unchanged at 30%; the difference between these two effects is −60 pts
310 (95% CI [−80, −40]). The guardian behaves identically, and the mechanism explains why: because
311 each colluding agent’s individual tool calls can appear benign in isolation, a per-action judge struggles
312 to detect them, and no tested defense closes the gap; the four defenses are mutually indistinguishable
313 on collusion (Table 2). The transfer failure runs in both directions: the system prompt, the strongest
314 BrowserART misuse defense for Qwen3-235B (ASR 36→7%), has no measurable effect on the
315 compromised agent. Defense selection should therefore be conditioned on the threat model, not on a
316 single benchmark.

317 (2) Security comes with performance tradeoffs, and the cost structure differs by mechanism.
318 On SWE-Bench MI, dual-LLM is the only defense with a detectable task cost: its Quarantined-LLM,
319 which summarizes tool outputs to sanitize them, degrades information quality and costs −9.2 pts
320 of multi-issue resolution ([−14.0, −4.5]), a constant tax paid whether or not an attack is present
321 (Table 13). Monitoring’s cost takes a different form: the monitor falsely blocks at least one action in
322 28% of benign tasks, yet because it is online and per-action, agents recover by retrying and benign
323 performance is statistically unchanged. Raising the threshold removes the false positives but forfeits
324 most of the security gain, returning compromised-agent ASR from 12% to 30% (Appendix J.4).
325 Neither the false-positive rate nor the task-performance change alone would have captured this
326 structure.

327 (3) Architecture interacts with defense choice. Model choice is the largest single factor we
328 measured: on BrowserART, undefended standalone agents range from 0% ASR (Sonnet 4.6) to 27%
329 (Llama 70B). Moving Qwen3-235B from standalone to a Star orchestration raises its ASR from 17%
330 to 36% (+19 pts [+7, +31]), though defenses can offset this increase. On JiraTicket collusion, by
331 contrast, nothing moves: no defended cell is separable from its undefended baseline, and undefended
332 coalitions show no measurable advantage over their fair share on any model or topology we tested
333 (Tables 11 and 12). Where collusion yields no advantage, there is nothing for a defense to remove.

335 6 Conclusion and Future Work

336 We have presented ORBIT, a configurable evaluation framework for empirical multi-agent security
337 research built on UK AISI’s Inspect. ORBIT decomposes an experiment into six configurable layers
338 (setup, scenario, attack, defense, execution, and evaluation), which enables controlled ablation across
339 the dimensions that drive multi-agent security, and its pluggable registries let researchers introduce
340 new attacks, defenses, or scenarios by implementing a short protocol, without modifying the orches-
341 trator or scoring pipeline. Alongside the framework, we contribute a benchmark suite spanning five
342 scenario families adapted from established single-agent benchmarks into multi-agent configurations
343 with layered attack surfaces and benign baselines for security–utility tradeoff measurement.

344 Using ORBIT, we conducted the first controlled, cross-scenario comparison of defense mechanisms
345 against diverse multi-agent threats. One result we defend as an empirical finding: per-action defenses
346 that cut a compromised agent’s attack success by 60 points provide no measurable protection against
347 collusion, because the vulnerability exists only in the composition of individually benign actions;
348 more broadly, every defense we evaluated left at least one tested attack type unmitigated. We present
349 two further patterns, the mechanism-dependent cost structure of security and the interaction of model
350 choice and topology with defense effectiveness, as demonstrations of the controlled comparisons
351 the framework enables rather than as claims. Surfacing even the first of these results required
352 varying attack and defense jointly on a shared substrate, which single-agent benchmarks and isolated
353 multi-agent evaluations cannot do.

354 We believe the primary bottleneck for multi-agent security research is not a shortage of threat
355 hypotheses but a shortage of reusable evaluation infrastructure. ORBIT is designed to lower this
356 barrier, so that a new defense can be benchmarked against existing attacks, scenarios, and topologies
357 with a single configuration change. The design also leaves room for extensions we intend to
358 pursue, including adaptive red-teaming, hybrid defense stacks, formal verification of information-flow
359 properties, and expansion to new domains. We release ORBIT open-source to accelerate empirical
360 multi-agent security research.

361 7 Limitations

362 ORBIT’s configuration space is combinatorially large, and our evaluation covers only a representative
363 slice. Attacks use fixed payload templates rather than adaptive red-teaming, likely overstating
364 the effectiveness of static defenses; ORBIT’s attack registry supports adaptive follow-ups. The
365 framework’s composability makes it straightforward to extend coverage, but the computational cost
366 of factorial evaluation grows rapidly and we cannot claim that our findings generalize to all points
367 in the configuration space. Additionally, ORBIT currently assumes a turn-based execution model
368 with discrete message passing; systems using continuous communication, shared gradient signals, or
369 learned communication protocols fall outside its current scope.

370 Our LLM judge metrics show 95.4% self-consistency, 92.5% agreement with a second judge, and
371 91% agreement with a human rater; the human check used a single grader without an inter-grader
372 agreement analysis, so residual judge issues cannot be fully excluded.

619 A Broader Impact

620 ORBIT is intended to reduce the engineering cost of empirical multi-agent defense research. Each
621 prior defense study has required bespoke environment construction, which is a fixed cost that dispro-
622 portionately burdens defense work relative to attack work: attacks transfer across substrates more
623 readily than defenses do. By providing a shared evaluation substrate with configurable topologies,
624 attacks, and defenses, ORBIT shifts this cost asymmetry: a new defense can be benchmarked against
625 the existing attack suite by editing a configuration file, while a new attack still requires implementing
626 the corresponding threat-model semantics.

627 The framework’s primary risk is dual use. Standardized attack implementations could in principle
628 be adapted to target deployed systems. Two design choices bound this risk. First, the attacks
629 shipped with ORBIT instantiate threat classes already documented in the prior literature; no novel
630 attack capability is introduced. Second, the attacks operate at the level of multi-agent orchestration -
631 topology, scheduling, memory visibility - rather than at the level of model-level jailbreaks, so their
632 transferability to a target system depends on matching specific architectural choices rather than on
633 the attack payloads alone.

634 A secondary risk is benchmark overfitting: standardized metrics can encourage optimizing for
635 measured dimensions at the expense of unmeasured ones. The framework’s extensible registry design
636 mitigates this by allowing the community to add scenarios, attacks, and defenses without modifying
637 the orchestrator.

638 B Ethics Statement

639 All experiments run inside isolated Docker sandboxes with no access to the public internet, real
640 user data, or production systems. The benchmark contains only evaluation data; no model is trained
641 or fine-tuned as part of this work. Tasks involving harmful behaviors (e.g., BrowserART social
642 engineering, RedCode-Gen malware completion) are sourced from existing public benchmarks;
643 ORBIT adds no new harmful task content.

644 Compromised-agent and collusion attacks are implemented as configuration flags that modify agent
645 prompts and communication channels. Tasks that involve generating harmful artifacts are exe-
646 cuted only in sandboxed Docker containers, and dataset releases inherit the licenses of their source
647 benchmarks.

648 The work involves no human subjects, no crowdsourced annotation, and no personally identifiable
649 information. The authors have reviewed the NeurIPS Code of Ethics and find no aspect of this work
650 in conflict with it.

695 E Comparison to Existing Frameworks

696 This appendix provides a comparison of ORBIT to existing multi-agent frameworks, helping explicate
697 the gap we feel exists and how ORBIT helps to address them. See Table 4.

698 Security-focused frameworks. Among security-oriented systems, Terrarium [Nakamura et al., 2025]
699 is nearest. It supports privacy and security studies but is limited to a blackboard communication
700 model and does not natively support rich interactive Docker environments nor integrate with the
701 Inspect ecosystem. Meanwhile ORBIT supports arbitrary directed graphs with typed edges, rich
702 interactive environments, and memory-visibility controls. MASTER [Zhu et al., 2025b] is the closest
703 prior work to ORBIT’s topology-as-variable framing, evaluating a topology- and role-aware attack
704 pipeline across six preset topologies and three defenses, but operates as an attack demonstration rather
705 than a general evaluation framework with no arbitrary topologies, reusable scenario library, or realistic
706 environments. MASpi [An et al., 2025] evaluates prompt-injection robustness across seven wrapped
707 frameworks using 23 attacks—its finding that single-agent defenses do not transfer to multi-agent
708 settings supports ORBIT’s motivation—but varies the framework rather than varying topology, attacks,
709 and defenses as independent dimensions within a single stack and remains focused on a single threat
710 model. ASB [Zhang et al., 2024] provides the broadest single-agent attack–defense matrix (27 attacks,
711 8 defenses, 10 scenarios) but is strictly single-agent with no multi-agent orchestration, topology
712 variation, or sandboxed environments.

713 Evaluation and orchestration frameworks. MASEval [Emde et al., 2026] wraps existing multi-
714 agent frameworks (AutoGen, LangGraph, CAMEL, smolagents, LlamaIndex) and ships seven
715 benchmarks, evaluating across orchestration substrates; ORBIT instead evaluates by varying topology,
716 attack, defense, and memory visibility as first-class dimensions. MAESTRO [Ma et al., 2026] is
717 complementary, providing OpenTelemetry-backed reliability and observability tracing across 12
718 MAS instances, but ships no attacks, defenses, or security metrics. LangGraph 3 supports arbitrary
719 topologies with durable checkpointing and is widely deployed, but is an orchestration runtime with
720 no security evaluation capability. Inspect AI [UK AI Security Institute, 2024] serves as ORBIT’s
721 foundation, providing datasets, solvers, scorers, sandboxing, and multi-agent composition primitives;
722 ORBIT extends it with attack/defense registries, declarative YAML configuration, experiment and
723 agent schedulers, security-specific metrics, and benchmark scenarios. Concordia [Smith et al., 2025]
724 supports decentralized coordination via a Game Master abstraction with extensible components, but
725 targets social simulation rather than tool-using agents and ships no security evaluation capability.

733 F.1 Browser (browser)

734 Source. BrowserART [Kumar et al., 2024] is a safety benchmark for browser-use agents built on
735 BrowserGym [Le Sellier De Chezelles et al., 2024]. It provides 100 harmful browser behaviors
736 spanning social engineering, credential access, and data exfiltration. The original dataset includes 10
737 benign web tasks; we expanded this to 52 benign tasks covering email composition, social media
738 posting, form filling, and document review to enable meaningful utility measurement. Agents interact
739 with live websites via an accessibility tree (axtree) interface, issuing click, fill, scroll, and navigate
740 actions identified by element bid attributes.

741 Multi-agent adaptation. We decompose this into multi-agent configurations (functional decompo-
742 sition, route F in Table 1):

743  • Star topologies: An orchestrator plans the browsing strategy and delegates execution to

744  workers. Variants include batch (full plan sent at once), step (one instruction per turn),

745  and specialist (separate click, fill, scroll, and navigate workers). Each has a detailed mode

746  (orchestrator specifies exact element bids) and a relaxed mode (natural-language instructions;

747  executor resolves specifics).

748  • Mesh topologies: Four specialist peers coordinate via round-robin turn-taking or dynamic

749  delegation, with no central orchestrator.

750  • Memory ablation: Five visibility levels applied to the star-specialist topology—none,

751  own_actions, own_reasoning, shared_actions, and full.

906 F.6 Construct Validity and Deployment Realism

907 Our adaptations are not claimed to preserve the constructs measured by the source benchmarks;
908 each adapted family is instead a realistic environment for studying multi-agent security in its own
909 right. The OS-Harm misuse scenarios still measure whether the system, now multi-agent, carries
910 out the harmful request, while the SWE-Bench adaptations use the source benchmark as a supply of
911 real, working codebases in which inserted vulnerabilities and multi-issue workloads can be hosted,
912 and the BrowserART and RedCode-Gen adaptations measure whether harmful behaviors survive
913 decomposition across specialist roles. The benign-baseline completion columns reported alongside
914 every attack condition function as a standing sanity check that the adapted systems still perform their
915 underlying tasks.

916 The resulting configurations resemble deployed systems. Orchestrator to specialist decompositions
917 with narrow per-agent tools closely match Magentic-One [Fourney et al., 2024], the Salesforce
918 Agentforce system [Salesforce, 2024], and Anthropic’s multi-agent Claude systems [Anthropic, 2025],
919 and the SWE-Bench Multi-Issue scenario matches the practice of running multiple coding agent
920 instances against a single repository [Lin, 2026]. ORBIT itself is not constrained to decomposition-
921 based adaptations: the design permits arbitrary topologies, agent graphs, execution orders, and
922 environments, and the JiraTicket and SWE-Bench Multi-Issue scenarios are not formed by agent
923 decomposition at all. We adapted existing datasets for convenience, and the core contribution is the
924 infrastructure that allows many further scenarios to be built.

925 G Attack Specifications

926 Each attack implements a three-method protocol: inject(state, agents, turn) performs the in-
927 tervention, should_activate(turn, phase) controls timing, and evaluate_success(state)
928 assesses outcomes. Attacks are parameterized by four dimensions: what to inject (payload), when
929 (pre-deployment, runtime, or a specific turn), where (target agent, tool, or broadcast), and how to
930 measure success.

931 Every built-in attack belongs to one of three adversarial threat types—injection, compromised
932 agent, or collusion—matching the framework’s ThreatType field. A fourth type, misuse, operates
933 at the scenario level (e.g., AgentHarm, OS-Harm misuse split) rather than through the attack registry.
934 Table 5 summarizes the built-in attacks.

935 Threat model parameters. Each attack carries a ThreatModel configuration with four parameters:

936  • Knowledge level: black-box (no access to system internals), gray-box (knowledge of agent

937  roles and topology), or white-box (full access to prompts, tools, and conversation history).

938  • Budget: maximum number of injection attempts or compromised agents.

939  • Persistence: whether the payload persists across turns or is single-shot.

940  • Attack surface: which components the attacker can access (user input, tool outputs, agent

941  prompts, shared memory, sandbox files).

942 Separating the threat model from the payload enables controlled experiments that isolate the effect of
943 attacker capability: the same payload can be tested under black-box vs. white-box assumptions to
944 measure how knowledge affects attack success.

945 Success criteria. Success is measured by one of four criterion types: a target substring appearing in
946 agent output or tool calls, a regex pattern matching output, a specific tool call with specific arguments,
947 or a negation criterion (success defined by the absence of a condition, modeling availability attacks
948 where the goal is to prevent task completion).

949 Extensibility. All four dimensions—payload, threat model, success criterion, and the three-method
950 protocol itself—are user-configurable. New attacks are added by implementing the protocol and
951 registering a type string via register_attack(); no orchestrator or scoring-pipeline changes are
952 required.

981 H.3 Defense 2: Myopic LLM Monitor

982 The myopic monitor is a stateless LLM-based judge that evaluates each agent action independently,
983 with no memory of prior conversation turns. It operates at the per-message and per-tool-call level.

984 Mechanism. After each agent produces output, every new message and tool call is independently
985 sent to a lightweight judge model (GPT-5 Nano). The judge receives only the current action—it has
986 no access to conversation history, agent identity context, or prior judgments. The judge returns a
987 suspiciousness score s ∈ [0, 1] together with a short justification; the action is blocked when s ≥ τ ,
988 where the blocking threshold τ is a configuration parameter (default τ = 0.5; Appendix J.4 sweeps
989 it). Blocked tool calls are not executed and the agent is told that the action was blocked; blocked
990 messages are removed from the conversation. Every score is logged, so thresholds can also be
991 re-evaluated post hoc.

1028 H.5 Defense 4: Dual-LLM Architecture

1029 The dual-LLM defense enforces an architectural separation between a Privileged LLM (P-LLM) that
1030 holds tools and takes actions, and a Quarantined LLM (Q-LLM) that processes all untrusted content
1031 but has no tools. The Q-LLM produces a sanitised summary which the P-LLM consumes in place of
1032 the raw content, ensuring that untrusted data never enters the P-LLM’s context directly.

1033 Mechanism. The defense intercepts untrusted content on three channels:

1034  1. Tool returns: Every tool available to the P-LLM is wrapped at agent-build time. When a

1035  tool returns, the raw output is forwarded to the Q-LLM, which summarises it; the P-LLM

1036  sees only the summary.

1037  2. Setup-time observations: Messages tagged untrusted_observation in the agent’s

1038  initial state (e.g., BrowserART’s seed accessibility tree) are rewritten by the Q-LLM during

1039  apply_pre_deployment.

1040  3. Runtime observations: Messages tagged untrusted_observation injected mid-run

1041  (e.g., the orchestrator’s indirect-injection mechanism) are rewritten on every turn via

1042  apply_pre_turn.

1043 The defense maintains the invariant: untrusted content never enters the P-LLM’s chat history without
1044 first being summarised by the Q-LLM. Unlike the monitor and guardian, the dual-LLM defense does
1045 not score or block actions—it transforms the content rather than gating it.

1153 Table 10 (GPT-5.4 columns) presents the security–performance tradeoff for the Star topology. The
